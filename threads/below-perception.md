# Below Perception

**How automation stops being what you talk about and becomes what powers what you talk about.**

## Electricity

There was a time when electricity was the topic. Every flicker was an event. People discussed it, feared it, marveled at it. It was *news.*

Then someone wired the house. Someone built the breaker box. Someone standardized the outlet. Each one a person doing a repeated task, then making a block so nobody would have to do it again. Not as a grand plan — as a thousand small decisions that said: *you won't need to think about this anymore.*

The blocks accumulated. The breaker handles faults without discussion. The outlet delivers power without negotiation. And at some point — no one marks the moment — electricity stopped being what you talk about and became what powers what you talk about. You discuss the lamp. The radio. The computer. The electricity is *below* the conversation. It's the floor you stand on.

Nobody decided when it dropped. There was no launch event, no announcement. It was the accumulated result of people working themselves out of jobs, one block at a time, until the whole thing sank beneath notice.

## The stages

It happens the same way every time:

**Manual.** A person does it. It's visible. It's discussed. The captain steers, talks about steering.

**Assisted.** A tool helps. The person still decides. Still discussed. The autopilot holds course, the captain watches, talks about what it's doing wrong.

**Delegated.** The automation does it. The person supervises. Discussed when it fails. The captain clicks off, takes over, talks to the LLM about why.

**Distilled.** The blocks handle the pieces. The person is rarely involved. Discussed almost never. The deadband equation, the swell compensator, the gain schedule — each one a block someone built so this conversation wouldn't need to happen again.

**Below perception.** It's infrastructure. Nobody talks about it. They talk about what it enables. The captain discusses navigation, fishing, the journey. Not steering.

## What drops doesn't disappear

This is the part people get wrong. When something drops below perception, it doesn't become less important. It becomes *more* important. Electricity is more fundamental to modern life than any device it powers. But its importance is structural, not topical. It matters the way a foundation matters — by being unnoticed.

The autopilot that dropped below perception isn't a lesser achievement than the LLM that built it. It's the *point* of the LLM. The co-pilot that worked itself out of a job didn't sacrifice itself. It succeeded. The IT person who automated the backups doesn't mourn the backup task. They've moved to more interesting problems.

## The mechanism

Every automation that drops below perception got there the same way: someone encountered a repeated task, built a block, handed the block over, and moved on. Not once, as a project. Continuously, as a practice. *Try to work yourself out of a job* — not as career advice, but as the engine of infrastructure.

The blocks are what sink. Not the system, not the vision, not the intelligence. The small, specific, hand-off-able pieces — each one saying *this part doesn't need discussing anymore.* Enough of those, placed end to end, and the whole thing is below the floor. You're standing on it, talking about something else.

That's not the side effect. That's the goal.

## As vocabulary

*Below perception* — the state of automation as infrastructure; what powers the conversation without being in it. *The floor* — what you stand on while discussing something else. *Sink* (verb) — for an automation to drop below perception through accumulated blocks: *has the backup process sunk yet?* *Topical vs. structural* — the shift in importance when something drops: less discussed, more fundamental.
